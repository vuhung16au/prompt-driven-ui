Syllabus

Four Pillars of Analysis

Volume I

Read the question

Before analyzing an argument, we must understand what question the text is answering. Misidentifying the core question leads all subsequent evaluations astray.

Learning Outcome Identify the core question hidden beneath rhetorical devices. Exercise: Find the central question in a selected passage from Seneca.
Volume II

Isolate arguments

Filter out narrative elements and emotional appeals to reveal the author's logical framework. A solid argument does not rely on the polish of its prose.

Learning Outcome Map out premises and conclusions. Exercise: Extract the main argument from an opinion editorial.
Volume III

Verify evidence

Not all evidence carries the same weight. Learn to categorize and evaluate the relevance and validity of cited data.

Learning Outcome Identify logical fallacies or weak evidence. Exercise: Evaluate citations in a research report.
Volume IV

Write responses

Present your agreement or disagreement in a structured, respectful, and logically rigorous manner.

Learning Outcome Draft a complete critical response. Exercise: Write a 300-word response to the analyzed text.
Practical Exercise I

Dissecting Arguments

Click on the sentence you believe contains the author's primary argument in the excerpt below.

Human memory is not akin to a tape recorder, passively capturing every sight and sound. Instead, it is an active reconstructive process. Understanding the fluid nature of memory helps us avoid regrettable errors in judgment.

Marginalia

Excerpt from the essay "Memory and Truth". Note how the author transitions from describing a phenomenon to proposing a course of action.

Correct

This sentence contains the main argument. The author uses the word "Therefore" to signal a conclusion (eyewitness testimony should not be absolute evidence) drawn from the premises (memory is reconstructive and fallible) stated earlier.

Incorrect

This sentence is a premise (explaining the mechanism of memory), not the primary argument. It serves to provide a reason leading to a more significant conclusion later in the paragraph.

The Moderator

Arthur Pendelton

As the facilitator of our discussions, the moderator's role is not to provide the correct answers, but to ask the right questions so participants can test the rigor of their own arguments.

  • I. No judgment of right/wrong, only demands for evidentiary clarity.
  • II. Maintains a dialogic space respectful of divergent perspectives.
  • III. Keeps the pace focused on intellectual depth rather than breadth.
